Raft consensus explained with five nodes

Consensus becomes easier to reason about when every timeout, vote and log entry has a node attached. Here is one five-node cluster, followed through success and failure.

By Gourav BarnwalPublished Sep 9, 2026Intermediate

What Raft is trying to preserve

Five servers—A, B, C, D and E—maintain copies of one ordered log. Clients send commands that change a state machine. Raft's job is to make the healthy servers apply the same committed commands in the same order despite delay, restart and network partition.

A · LEADER
B · FOLLOWER
C · FOLLOWER
D · FOLLOWER
E · FOLLOWER

A five-node cluster needs a majority of three. It can therefore continue after two node failures, provided the remaining three can communicate. The majority rule is not a performance optimization; overlapping majorities are the bridge that prevents two conflicting histories from both becoming committed.

Roles and terms

Each server is a follower, candidate or leader. Time is divided into monotonically increasing terms. A term usually starts with an election and has at most one leader accepted by a majority.

  • Follower: accepts leader replication and waits for heartbeats.
  • Candidate: increments its term, votes for itself and requests votes after an election timeout.
  • Leader: accepts client commands, appends them to its log and replicates them to followers.

A server that sees a higher term updates its term and becomes a follower. Terms let the cluster recognize that older leadership information is stale.

Leader election, step by step

Assume all nodes begin as followers in term 7 and no leader is active.

  1. A's randomized election timeout expires first.
  2. A advances to term 8, becomes a candidate, votes for itself and sends vote requests.
  3. B and C have not voted in term 8 and consider A's log at least as up to date as their own, so they grant votes.
  4. With votes from A, B and C, A has a majority and becomes leader for term 8.
  5. A immediately sends heartbeats, causing D and E to learn the current term and leader.

Randomized timeouts reduce the chance that several candidates begin together. If A and B split votes and nobody reaches three, the term ends without a leader; a later timeout starts a new term and another vote.

Replicating and committing one command

A client asks the cluster to apply set x = 42. Leader A appends a new entry to its local log at index 12 in term 8, then sends AppendEntries requests containing the previous log index and term.

B and C verify that their logs match at the previous position, append index 12 and acknowledge. Once A knows that the term-8 entry exists on a majority—A, B and C—it advances its commit index. A applies the command to its state machine and returns success. Later heartbeats tell followers the new commit index so they apply it too.

Replication means copies exist. Commitment means a quorum and the Raft rules make the entry safe to apply.

The leader becomes isolated

Now A is separated from B, C, D and E. It may still believe it is leader, but it cannot replicate a new entry to a majority and therefore cannot commit new work.

Among the connected four nodes, D times out, starts term 9 and wins votes from D, B and C. D becomes leader. If a client reaches D, the group can continue. When the network heals, A sees term 9, steps down and accepts D's authority.

Any uncommitted suffix created only on isolated A may be overwritten to match D's log. A client must not receive a success response for such an entry, because A never proved majority replication.

How inconsistent logs converge

The leader tracks a next index for each follower. If a follower rejects AppendEntries because the previous index or term does not match, the leader backs up to find a common prefix. It then sends the missing suffix. Conflicting uncommitted entries on the follower are removed.

The vote rule also checks log freshness. A candidate whose last log term is older—or whose term matches but last index is shorter—cannot win a vote from a more up-to-date server. This blocks a server missing committed history from becoming leader through timing alone.

The safety rules worth remembering

  • A server votes for at most one candidate in a term.
  • A leader never overwrites its own log; it appends new entries.
  • Matching index and term imply matching preceding history.
  • A committed entry remains present in future leaders.
  • State machines apply only committed entries and apply them in log order.

Raft separates safety from availability. A minority partition may stop serving writes even when its machines are healthy. That is the correct result when the alternative is two accepted histories.

What the algorithm does not solve for you

A production Raft-backed system still needs durable storage, snapshots, log compaction, membership changes, client retry semantics, read-consistency choices, backpressure and observability. It also needs realistic failure testing: delayed messages, disk stalls and clock-related timeout behavior are often more revealing than simply killing a process.

The most useful operational signals include current term, leader changes, election duration, replication lag per follower, commit-index movement, snapshot progress and request latency separated by leader and follower paths.
